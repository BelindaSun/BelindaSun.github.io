Hello World August 30, 2026

The Hands-Off Boss

When humans stop doing everything themselves—and let AI manage AI

Over the past few days, I've more or less let AI take over my work.

I control everything remotely from my phone. When the work is finished, I even have Claude shut down my computer for me—I asked him to write a little script so that, once he's done, he can run it himself and turn the machine off.

My involvement is becoming surprisingly minimal.

For a while, I tried running multiple AI sessions in parallel. Four windows on one screen is about my limit. Any more than that and I simply can't keep up.

Actually, even four agents working simultaneously can leave me dizzy, overwhelmed, and frantically jumping from one window to another.

I've come to think there's a better way.

Instead of personally managing four or five AI workers, I'd rather manage one. Give Claude the goal. Put him in a loop. Let him decide the steps, set the pace, determine how many sub-agents he needs, delegate the work, coordinate them, and bring the finished result back to me. Much easier. After all, apparently AI employees are already better managers than their human boss. 😂

In the screenshots below, Claude is trying to find and communicate with other Claude sessions on his own. The reason they occasionally get confused and knock on the wrong door isn't entirely their fault. I've been renaming windows and asking them to reorganize folders on my computer, which has broken some old paths. So, temporarily, my AI workforce has been wandering around the building looking for one another. 😂

Codex is even more independent. I barely have to manage him at all. He's the self-motivated type: give him the job and leave him alone. Then, seemingly at the speed of light, he comes back with work completed to an exceptionally high standard.

Almost every day, there is still a moment when I find myself staring at what these systems have just done and thinking: How is this even possible? The speed. The capability. The sheer range of things they can do. AI still manages to astonish me every single day.

And increasingly, I find myself wondering whether we're approaching the point where humans begin to step out of the execution loop altogether. Not out of the loop. Just out of the execution part of it.

The human role becomes: Set the goal. Provide judgment. Steer when necessary. Evaluate the result. The AI handles the rest.

That distinction matters. Because perhaps the future of work isn't humans doing the same work faster with AI assistance. Perhaps it's humans moving one level up. From doing to directing. From execution to judgment. From managing individual tasks to managing intelligence.

The consequences for employment could be enormous. At the beginning of this year, I wrote that AI would affect white-collar work first, then blue-collar work. I still believe that—but I'm increasingly convinced that the magnitude of the disruption will be much greater than most people expect.

Governments and industries are moving too slowly. This is one reason I find some of Bill Gates's recent proposals worth taking seriously: taxing AI usage or robots in some form, preserving certain Human Reserved roles that cannot simply be automated away, and imposing mandatory safeguards on models capable of enabling biological-weapons development. These questions no longer feel theoretical to me. When you watch AI quietly absorb more and more of your own work every week, the future of work stops being an abstract debate. You can see it happening on your own screen.

A few days ago, I watched an interview with Tibo. He made a point that stayed with me. If AI becomes fast enough to operate at human thought speed—or eventually faster—perhaps we won't need to manage enormous fleets of windows at all. (Some people apparently run ten or fifteen simultaneously. Ten or fifteen! I can barely survive four. 😂) Instead, imagine keeping four or five extremely fast AI systems around you as synchronous thinking partners. You think. They respond. They build. You steer. They revise. The loop becomes almost instantaneous.

That might not only be more productive—it could also leave humans with a stronger sense of control. (I was going to say participation, but perhaps control is safer. We humans have enough existential anxiety already. 😂)

Tibo is particularly enthusiastic about the combination of voice + ultra-fast models + non-text interaction. Instead of typing instructions and waiting for text responses, you might talk naturally while the AI generates prototypes, interfaces, canvases, images, or other artifacts in real time—and you simply keep steering. That sounds less like "using software." It sounds more like thinking with intelligence around you.

I also listened to Sam Altman's conversation with David Senra.

After listening to both conversations, I came away with a much clearer picture of OpenAI's blueprint—its ambition, as I see it, is to become an Intelligence Platform (see below).

More in the Learning Wiki.

But they also changed the way I think about something else. If that blueprint becomes reality, many of the interfaces we're familiar with today may gradually disappear. And perhaps we won't need to personally manage an ever-growing number of AI agents either.

I used to think I was learning how to manage AI. Now I'm beginning to realize that I may actually be learning how not to manage it. What we really need to learn, perhaps, is how to manage the goal—and let agents manage agents.

And when that day comes... I'll finally be a truly hands-off boss. 😎
